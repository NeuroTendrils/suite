<Hook>
Brain signals are only microvolts. A single blink can be ten times bigger. How do you tell what came from the brain?
</Hook>

## Not everything on an EEG is brain

An **artifact** is any part of a recording that did not come from the brain. Because brain signals are so small, almost anything electrical near the electrodes can swamp them.

Artifacts come from two places:

- **Your body**: eyes, muscles, and heart all produce electrical fields of their own
- **Your equipment and surroundings**: loose electrodes, cables, and building wiring

## Artifacts from the body

**Eye blinks.** The eyeball is an electrical dipole: the front (cornea) is positive relative to the back (retina). Each blink shifts that dipole, producing a large, slow hump that is biggest on the forehead channels (**Fp1**, **Fp2**) and fades toward the back of the head.

**Muscle tension (EMG).** Muscle fibres fire much faster than cortex, so clenching the jaw or frowning adds dense, fast “fuzz”. It is strongest on the temporal channels (**T7**, **T8**), which sit over the jaw muscles.

**Heartbeat (ECG).** The heart is the strongest electrical generator in the body. Its field can reach the scalp as sharp, evenly spaced spikes about once a second.

## Artifacts from equipment

**Mains interference.** Building wiring carries alternating current at **60 Hz** in North America (50 Hz in much of the world). It shows up as a perfectly regular oscillation with the same strength on every channel.

**A loose electrode.** When an electrode loses contact, its voltage jumps suddenly. Real brain activity is spread across neighbouring electrodes, so a jump on one channel alone is a warning sign.

## Why this matters for BCIs

Researchers handle artifacts by **rejecting** bad stretches of data, **filtering** (for example, a notch filter at 60 Hz), or separating sources with techniques like **independent component analysis**. None of these work unless someone first recognizes what they are looking at.

<Callout kind="myth">
A BCI that “reads concentration” may actually be reading your forehead muscles. If people frown when they focus, a decoder can learn the frown. Always check what a model is really responding to.
</Callout>
