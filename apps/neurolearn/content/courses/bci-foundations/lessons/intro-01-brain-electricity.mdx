<Hook>
One neuron’s electrical field is tiny. So how can a headset on your scalp measure “brain activity” at all?
</Hook>

## Neurons store electrical energy

Neurons use **sodium–potassium pumps** to push sodium ions out of the cell and pull potassium ions in. Because potassium can leak back out through open channels, the inside of the cell ends up about **−70 mV** relative to the outside. That charge separation is called **polarization**, and it is stored electrical potential energy.

When enough input arrives, **sodium channels open** and the inside swings briefly positive. That ~1 ms spike, the **action potential**, travels down the **axon** to the **synapse**, where neurotransmitters cross the gap and nudge the next cell’s voltage up or down.

<Callout kind="tip">
Think of a neuron like a charged battery that can fire a fast electrical pulse, then reset.
</Callout>

## What EEG actually picks up

You might expect EEG to record those spikes. Mostly, it doesn’t.

Action potentials are very brief, and nearby cells rarely fire at exactly the same moment, so their fields largely **cancel out**. Scalp EEG mainly reflects **postsynaptic potentials**: slower currents (tens of milliseconds) that flow in a neuron’s dendrites after neurotransmitters arrive.

The key players are **pyramidal neurons** in the cortex. They stand side by side like trees in a forest, all pointing toward the surface. When thousands of them receive **synchronized** input, their small currents line up and add into one field big enough to reach the scalp.

## Tissue dims and blurs the message

Even that summed field is weakened on the way out:

- **Cerebrospinal fluid** conducts well, so current spreads out sideways instead of heading straight to the scalp
- **Skull bone** conducts poorly, which weakens the signal and smears it across a wide patch of scalp
- **Skin and hair** add more distance between the source and the electrode

By the time activity reaches the scalp, it is only **microvolts** (millionths of a volt), and each electrode hears a blend of many centimetres of cortex.

<Callout kind="limit">
Real heads vary in skull thickness and tissue properties, so the same brain activity can look different on two people. The key idea: the physics between cortex and electrode is lossy and blurry.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="myth">
EEG does not cleanly “see” one thought from one neuron. It sees blurred, weakened echoes of many cells receiving input together.
</Callout>
